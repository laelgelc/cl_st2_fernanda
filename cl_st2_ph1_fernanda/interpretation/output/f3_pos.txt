## Factor f3 (positive): **Evaluating responses to contextualised exam tasks**

The positive pole is best interpreted as the discourse of reporting how students respond to exam tasks set in concrete situations. It combines the vocabulary of the situations students are asked to interpret with the report’s practice of comparing their answers with an expected response. The strongest evidence for this combination comes from the 2019 technical analyses.

**Corpus-wide loadings.** Several of the strongest words evoke situations involving actors, decisions, and consequences: *consultor* (1.12), *autorizar* (1.10), *beneficiário* (1.10), *assessora* (.98), and *compensação* (.98). Others suggest events and their explanation: *brusco* (1.13), *causa-efeito* (1.10), *catástrofe* (1.06), *deslizamento* (.41), and *causalidade* (.32). Words such as *localizar* (.75), *compreensível* (.73), and *julgar* (.65) are compatible with interpreting a task or evaluating a response, although the loadings alone cannot establish their exact function. The range from *carbono* and *dióxido* to *vacinar* and *caminhada* suggests varied task settings rather than one subject-matter theme.

**What the examples show.** All ten excerpts come from `2019_c6` and score 17–18. Their openings present descriptive statistics, item difficulty and discrimination, and score distributions. But they also explain that common answers to open-ended questions are compared with the *padrão de resposta esperado* and that responses to the two General Education questions receive language-correction commentary. That comparison gives the pole its clearest discourse function. The full-document word lists supply the lexical connection: all ten reports share terms such as *consultor, autorizar, beneficiário, causa-efeito, catástrofe, compreensível,* and *julgar* despite covering different courses. Some add terms such as *fórmula*, *carbono/dióxido*, or *vacinar*. This cross-course recurrence is consistent with shared exam material or commentary, alongside course-specific content. The excerpts do not show whether individual words occur in prompts, answer standards, or commentary, so the pole should not be labelled simply **statistical performance reporting** on the basis of the visible openings.

**Groups driving the pole.** `2019_c6` is the clear driver (mean **16.14**, N = 29, SD = 0.83), well ahead of `2022_c6` (**5.58**), `2014_c6` (**4.78**), and `2021_c6` (**3.95**). The low within-group spread in `2019_c6` shows that the concentration extends beyond the ten selected examples. The pattern is edition-sensitive rather than a steady increase or a uniform feature of c6: by 2023, c6 is at **1.30**, while c5 reaches **3.81**, its highest mean. c4 is generally weak, peaking at **2.00** in 2023. The c5 scores show that positive scoring is not exclusive to technical analysis, but without c5 excerpts they do not establish that the same discourse practice accounts for those scores.

**Label options**
- **Evaluating responses to contextualised exam tasks** — preferred because it captures both the scenario-based vocabulary and the report’s comparison of student answers with expected answers.
- **Contextualised item analysis** — a shorter label that leaves open where the scenario vocabulary occurs, but makes the evaluation of student responses less explicit.
